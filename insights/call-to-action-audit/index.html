The Call to Action Audit: Why Your CTAs Are Not Working and How to Fix Them

Most small business websites have calls to action that nobody clicks. Here is how to audit yours, fix what is broken, and start converting visitors into real enquiries.

MS
Martin Spooner Founder, MdS Websites  ·  August 2026

A weak call to action on a website costs UK small businesses enquiries every single day. If your CTA says "Contact us" or "Learn more," you are almost certainly leaving money on the table. Research from Unbounce, analysing over 74,000 landing pages, found that personalised, specific CTAs convert up to 202% better than generic ones. This guide walks you through a practical call to action audit you can complete in 30 minutes, with fixes you can apply the same afternoon.

What is a call to action and why does it matter so much?

A call to action is the moment on your website where you ask the visitor to do something specific: call you, fill in a form, book a consultation, request a quote. It is the bridge between someone browsing your site and someone becoming a customer. According to HubSpot's 2024 marketing statistics, personalised CTAs perform 202% better than default versions. Yet most small business websites in the UK treat CTAs as an afterthought, a button labelled "Submit" tucked at the bottom of a page nobody scrolls to.

I have audited hundreds of small business websites over the years. The single most common problem is not bad design or poor SEO. It is that the website never actually asks the visitor to do anything specific. The business owner assumes people will find the contact page on their own. They will not.

If your website does not tell visitors what to do next, they will do the easiest thing available: leave.

A strong call to action on a website removes ambiguity. It tells the visitor exactly what happens when they click, what it will cost them (ideally nothing), and why it is worth their time.

How do you know if your CTAs are failing?

Before you fix anything, you need to diagnose the problem. A failing CTA shows clear symptoms, and most of them are visible in Google Analytics 4 or simply by looking at your own site with fresh eyes. The average website conversion rate for UK small businesses sits between 2% and 5%, according to Contentsquare's 2024 Digital Experience Benchmark report. If yours is below 2%, your CTAs are a prime suspect.

Here is a quick diagnostic you can run right now:

  • Check your bounce rate. If visitors are landing on key pages and leaving without clicking anything, your CTA is not compelling enough, or it is not visible enough.
  • Count your CTAs per page. Open your homepage. How many different actions are you asking the visitor to take? If the answer is more than two, you have a competition problem.
  • Read your button text out loud. Does it tell the visitor what they will get? "Submit" tells them nothing. "Get my free quote in 24 hours" tells them everything.
  • Check mobile. Open every key page on your phone. Can you see a CTA without scrolling? Can you tap it easily with your thumb? Google's mobile usability data shows that 61% of users are unlikely to return to a mobile site they had trouble accessing.

If any of those checks raised a flag, keep reading. The fixes are straightforward.

What makes a call to action actually work?

The difference between a CTA that converts and one that gets ignored comes down to four things: clarity, specificity, placement, and reduced risk. None of them require a redesign. Most require nothing more than changing a few words and moving a button.

Clarity. The visitor should understand exactly what happens when they click. "Book a free 20-minute website review" is clear. "Get in touch" is vague. Vague loses.

Specificity. Include details that answer the visitor's unspoken questions. How long will it take? What will it cost? What will they receive? The UK Government Digital Service recommends using specific, active language in all user-facing content because it reduces cognitive load and increases action.

Placement. Your primary CTA should appear above the fold on every key page. It should also repeat at least once further down the page for visitors who scroll before deciding. Research from the Nielsen Norman Group confirms that content placed above the fold receives 84% more attention than content below it.

Reduced risk. Every CTA should lower the barrier to action. Phrases like "no obligation," "free," "takes 2 minutes," or "no credit card required" address the hesitation that stops people clicking. You are not being pushy. You are being helpful.

I have seen businesses increase their enquiry rate by 40% or more just by changing a button from "Contact us" to "Get your free quote, no obligation." Same page, same design, same traffic. Different words, different result.

The 30-minute CTA audit: a step-by-step guide

You can audit every call to action on your website in half an hour. Here is exactly how to do it, page by page.

Step 1: List every page that matters. Open a spreadsheet. List your homepage, each service page, your about page, and your contact page. These are the pages where CTAs need to work hardest. Ignore your blog archive and legal pages for now.

Step 2: Screenshot each page on mobile and desktop. For each page, take a screenshot of the first screen (above the fold) on both your phone and your desktop. This gives you an honest picture of what visitors actually see first.

Step 3: Answer five questions for each page.

  1. Is there a visible CTA above the fold? (Yes or no.)
  2. Does the button text say what the visitor will get? (Not "Submit" or "Click here.")
  3. Is there only one primary CTA competing for attention? (Multiple CTAs dilute each other.)
  4. Does the CTA repeat further down the page? (Visitors who scroll need a second chance.)
  5. Is there a risk-reducer near the CTA? ("Free," "no obligation," "takes 2 minutes.")

Step 4: Score each page. Give one point for each "yes." Any page scoring below 4 out of 5 needs attention. Start with your homepage and your most visited service page.

Step 5: Rewrite and reposition. For every page that scored poorly, rewrite the CTA using the clarity, specificity, placement, and risk-reduction principles above. You do not need a developer for this. Most website platforms let you edit button text and move sections in minutes.

What are the most common CTA mistakes on UK small business websites?

After reviewing websites across trades, hospitality, and professional services, I see the same mistakes repeated constantly. Every one of them is fixable.

"Contact us" as the only CTA. This is the most widespread problem. "Contact us" gives the visitor no reason to act now and no idea what will happen when they do. Replace it with something specific: "Book a free 15-minute call" or "Request your quote today."

Too many competing actions. "Call us, email us, follow us on Instagram, download our guide, sign up for our newsletter." When you offer five options, you are really offering zero. The visitor freezes. Pick one primary action per page.

CTAs hidden below the fold. If your first CTA appears only after three sections of text, most visitors will never see it. The data is clear: above-the-fold content gets dramatically more engagement. Put your strongest CTA in the hero section.

No CTA on the services page. This one surprises people, but it is common. A visitor reads about your services, feels interested, and then has nowhere obvious to go. Every service page needs a CTA at the top and at the bottom, minimum.

Generic button styling. A CTA that looks like every other link on the page will be ignored. It needs to stand out visually: contrasting colour, adequate size, clear spacing. The button should be the most obvious element on the screen.

How do CTAs fit into your wider conversion strategy?

A call to action does not work in isolation. It is the final step in a chain that starts with the visitor landing on your page and feeling understood. Your headline needs to speak to their problem. Your content needs to build trust. Your social proof needs to remove doubt. And then, when the visitor is ready, your CTA needs to make the next step feel easy and risk-free.

Think of it this way: if your homepage copy is weak, no CTA in the world will save it. But if your copy is strong and your CTA is weak, you are doing all the hard work and fumbling at the finish line.

This is why we approach every web design project as a conversion exercise, not a design exercise. The visual design supports the message, and the message builds towards the action. Get that sequence right and the enquiries follow.

If your brand messaging is unclear before you even get to the CTA stage, it may be worth looking at your branding foundations first. A confused brand creates confused visitors, and confused visitors do not click anything.

How should you test your CTAs after making changes?

Making changes is only half the job. You need to measure whether those changes are working. Here is a simple approach that does not require expensive software.

Track clicks in Google Analytics 4. Set up event tracking on your CTA buttons. GA4 makes this relatively straightforward with its enhanced measurement features. You want to know how many people see the CTA versus how many click it.

Run an A/B test with two button texts. If your website platform supports it, try running your original CTA text against your new version for two weeks. Even a small business site with 500 monthly visitors can produce a meaningful signal over 30 days.

Ask real people. Show your page to five people who match your target customer. Ask them: "What would you do on this page?" If they hesitate or look for the CTA, you have more work to do.

Monitor your enquiry volume. The ultimate test is simple. Are you getting more enquiries than before? Track form submissions and phone calls weekly. A 20% increase in enquiries from the same traffic is a clear sign your CTA changes are working.

Frequently asked questions

How many calls to action should a page have? One primary CTA per page, repeated two or three times at different scroll points. You can have a secondary action (like a phone number in the header), but the main CTA should be visually dominant and consistent throughout the page.

What is the best colour for a CTA button? There is no universal "best colour." The most important thing is contrast: your CTA button should be the most visually distinct element on the page. If your site uses blue tones throughout, an orange or green button will stand out. HubSpot's testing found that red buttons outperformed green in one test, but the real variable was contrast against the surrounding design, not the colour itself.

Should I use "Click here" as CTA text? No. "Click here" tells the visitor nothing about what happens next. It also creates accessibility problems, as screen readers read link text out of context. Use descriptive, action-oriented text: "Get your free quote," "Book a consultation," or "See our work."

How often should I update my CTAs? Review them every three to six months, or whenever you notice a drop in enquiries. Seasonal changes, new services, or shifts in your target audience are all good triggers for a CTA refresh. Stale CTAs can quietly underperform without you noticing.

Do pop-up CTAs work for small business websites? They can, but they must be used carefully. Google's intrusive interstitial guidelines penalise pop-ups that block content on mobile, particularly for pages arriving from search. A small slide-in after 30 seconds of browsing can work well. A full-screen pop-up that appears before the visitor has read a single word will drive people away.

What is the difference between a CTA and a contact page? A CTA is a prompt that appears across your site, directing visitors towards a specific action. A contact page is a destination. Your CTAs should link to your contact page (or embed a form), but they are not the same thing. A strong CTA makes the visitor want to visit the contact page. A weak one means they never get there.
