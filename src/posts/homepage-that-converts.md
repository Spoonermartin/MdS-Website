Most small business homepages describe the business. The best ones guide the visitor towards a decision within seconds. Good homepage copywriting for a small business is not about clever words or polished marketing speak. It is about structure, clarity, and removing every reason a visitor might hit the back button. According to Google's own research, it takes just 50 milliseconds for a user to form an opinion about a website. This guide covers the practical steps that turn a passive homepage into one that generates enquiries.

---

## Why does homepage copywriting matter more than design?

Design matters, but words do the heavy lifting. A beautifully designed homepage with vague copy ("Welcome to our website. We are passionate about delivering excellence.") converts worse than a plain page with a clear, specific message. Research from the Nielsen Norman Group found that users spend an average of 5.59 seconds looking at written content on a page, compared to 5.94 seconds on images. The gap is negligible, but the point is significant: visitors read before they act.

I have reviewed hundreds of small business websites over the years, and the pattern is consistent. The sites that generate enquiries are not the ones with the flashiest animations or the most expensive photography. They are the ones where a visitor can answer three questions within five seconds: What does this business do? Is it for someone like me? What should I do next?

> Good homepage copy does not describe what you do. It tells the visitor what you can do for them.

If your homepage fails on any of those three, you are losing potential customers before they have even seen your services page. See our [guide to turning visitors into enquiries](/insights) for the broader picture.

## What should the first sentence on your homepage say?

The first sentence should state who you help and what outcome you deliver. Not your company history. Not your mission statement. Not "Welcome to." According to the Behavioural Insights Team, people make faster decisions when the most relevant information appears first, a principle they call "front-loading." Your homepage headline and opening line are the front-load moment.

Here is a simple test. Read the first sentence on your current homepage out loud. If it starts with the name of your business, you have already wasted your most valuable real estate on something the visitor can see in your logo.

**Instead of:** "Smith & Co Plumbing has been serving the Cambridge area since 2004."

**Try:** "Emergency plumber in Cambridge. Same-day callouts, no call-out fee, five-star reviews."

The second version answers every question a panicking homeowner is asking. The first version answers a question nobody asked.

**A practical formula that works:**

* **Who you help** + **what outcome they get** + **where you operate**
* "We build websites for trades businesses across the UK that generate enquiries, not just look good."
* "Independent financial advice for families in Cambridgeshire, with no hidden fees."

This is not clever copywriting. It is clarity. And clarity converts.

## How should you structure a homepage that generates enquiries?

A homepage that converts follows a predictable structure. According to research by the Baymard Institute, 18% of users abandon a site due to poor navigation or confusing layout. The structure below addresses that directly. Every section earns the right to exist by moving the visitor one step closer to action.

**Section 1: Hero (above the fold)**
Your headline, a one-line supporting statement, and a single call to action. Nothing else. No sliders, no auto-playing videos, no "latest news" widget. One message, one action.

**Section 2: The problem you solve**
Two or three sentences acknowledging the pain point your ideal customer has right now. This is where empathy earns trust. "Losing bookings to Booking.com?" is more powerful than "We offer bespoke hospitality websites."

**Section 3: Social proof**
Testimonials, Google review stars, client logos, or a short case study result. Position this early, not at the bottom. BrightLocal's 2024 survey found that 49% of consumers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations. Your reviews need to appear before doubt sets in.

**Section 4: Services overview**
Three or four cards linking to your main service pages. Brief descriptions, not essays. Each card answers "what is this and who is it for?"

**Section 5: Credibility markers**
Certifications, years of experience, number of clients served, industry memberships. Specific numbers beat vague claims every time. "14 years, 200+ projects" is stronger than "years of experience."

**Section 6: Call to action**
Repeat your primary CTA. If the visitor has scrolled this far, they are interested. Make it easy. A short form, a phone number, or a booking link.

I have seen businesses double their enquiry rate simply by moving their testimonials from the footer to the second section on the page. It is not magic. It is sequencing.

## What are the most common homepage copywriting mistakes?

Small business homepages fail in predictable ways. These are the mistakes I encounter most often, and every one of them is fixable in an afternoon.

**Writing about yourself instead of the customer.** Count the number of times your homepage says "we" versus "you." If "we" wins, the page is about you, not about the person reading it. Flip the ratio.

**Using jargon your customers do not use.** If you are a web designer writing "bespoke digital solutions leveraging cutting-edge frameworks," you have lost every small business owner who just wants more phone calls. Write in the language your customers actually use. The UK Government's own digital service standard mandates a reading age of nine for public-facing content. Your homepage should aim for similar clarity.

**Having no clear call to action.** Or worse, having five competing ones. "Call us, email us, download our guide, follow us on LinkedIn, subscribe to our newsletter." When everything competes, nothing wins. Pick one primary action per page.

**Burying the phone number.** For service businesses, the phone number should be visible without scrolling on every device. On mobile, it should be tappable. I have audited sites where finding the phone number required three taps and a scroll. That is three opportunities for the visitor to give up.

**Slow load times.** Google's Core Web Vitals data shows that 53% of mobile visitors abandon a site that takes longer than three seconds to load. Your copy might be perfect, but if the page takes five seconds to appear, most visitors will never read it.

## How do you write calls to action that people actually click?

A call to action is not a button label. It is a promise. The difference between "Submit" and "Get your free quote" is the difference between a form that converts at 2% and one that converts at 5%. CTA wording has been studied extensively; Unbounce's analysis of over 74,000 landing pages found that action-oriented, benefit-driven CTAs outperform generic ones by up to 30%.

**Rules that work for small business homepages:**

* **Be specific.** "Book a free 20-minute website review" beats "Contact us." The visitor knows exactly what happens next.
* **Reduce perceived risk.** Add "no obligation," "free," or "takes 2 minutes" near the button. You are lowering the barrier.
* **Use first person where appropriate.** "Get my free quote" can outperform "Get your free quote" because it makes the action feel owned.
* **Repeat the CTA.** Once in the hero, once at the bottom, and once in the middle if the page is long. Do not assume visitors scroll linearly.

The biggest mistake I see: CTAs that say "Learn more." Learn more about what? Every CTA should tell the visitor what they will get and what it will cost them (ideally nothing but a few minutes of their time).

## How do you test whether your homepage is working?

You do not need expensive tools or a marketing degree. You need five minutes, a phone, and some honesty. Here is a practical audit you can run right now.

1. **The five-second test.** Show your homepage to someone who has never seen it. After five seconds, ask: "What does this business do? Who is it for? What should you do next?" If they cannot answer all three, your copy needs work.
2. **The mobile test.** Open your homepage on your phone. Can you see a phone number without scrolling? Can you tap it to call? Is the text readable without zooming? Is the main CTA visible on the first screen?
3. **The jargon test.** Read your homepage copy to a friend who works in a completely different industry. Every phrase they do not immediately understand is a phrase that is costing you visitors.
4. **The "so what?" test.** Read every sentence on your homepage. After each one, ask "so what?" If you cannot connect it directly to a benefit the visitor cares about, cut it or rewrite it.
5. **Check your analytics.** Google Analytics 4 shows your homepage bounce rate. If more than 60% of visitors leave without clicking anything, your homepage is not doing its job. The UK average for small business websites sits around 45-55%, according to Contentsquare's 2024 Digital Experience Benchmark report.

These are not theoretical exercises. I run variations of them on every project we take on at [MdS Websites](/portfolio), and they consistently reveal opportunities that were invisible to the business owner.

## What about SEO on your homepage?

Your homepage should target your single most important keyword phrase, and it should appear naturally in three places: the page title tag, the H1 heading, and the first paragraph. For a local business, that phrase almost always includes your location. "Cambridge web design" or "plumber in Ely" tells Google exactly what you do and where.

Do not stuff keywords. Google's helpful content guidelines penalise pages written for search engines rather than people. Write for the human first, then check that your target phrase appears naturally two or three times.

Your meta description (the snippet shown in search results) should be 150-160 characters, include your primary keyword, and give a clear reason to click. Think of it as a tiny advert for your homepage.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should homepage copy be for a small business website?**
There is no magic number, but most effective small business homepages run between 400 and 800 words. Enough to answer the key questions (what, who, why, what next) without overwhelming the visitor. Shorter is fine if every word earns its place. Longer is fine if the content is genuinely useful.

**Should I hire a copywriter for my homepage?**
If you can afford it, yes. Good homepage copywriting for a small business pays for itself through increased enquiries. If budget is tight, follow the structure in this guide, write a first draft yourself, and ask three people outside your industry to read it. Their confusion will show you where to improve.

**How often should I update my homepage?**
Review it every six months at minimum. If your services, pricing, or customer base have changed, update sooner. Stale homepages with outdated information erode trust. Google also favours fresh content, so regular updates help with search rankings.

**What is the single biggest homepage mistake small businesses make?**
Writing about themselves instead of the customer. Your homepage is not your autobiography. It is a conversation with someone who has a problem and wants to know if you can solve it. Lead with their problem, not your history.

**Does my homepage need video?**
It can help, but only if the video is short (under 90 seconds), loads fast, and adds something the text does not. A talking-head welcome video that repeats the homepage copy is wasted bandwidth. A 60-second walkthrough of a completed project or a client testimonial can be genuinely powerful.

**How do I know if my homepage is converting well?**
Set up goal tracking in Google Analytics 4. Track form submissions, phone number clicks, and booking link clicks as conversion events. If your homepage conversion rate is below 2%, there is room to improve. Most small business homepages convert between 2% and 5% of visitors into some form of action.
